How to rank higher on Google Maps

Google ranks local results on three factors. Here is which ones you can influence, what to do about each, and what to leave alone.

Updated 25 November 2026 · By the BabyRock team

The short answer

Google ranks local results on relevance, distance and prominence. You can't change distance, and you can't pay for a better position. You can improve relevance with a complete, accurate profile and prominence with more reviews, good ratings, helpful replies and a consistent online presence. Keyword-stuffed names and fake reviews can get you suspended.

The steps

  1. Complete every fieldFill in categories, hours, address, phone, website, services and description so Google can match you to more searches.
  2. Choose accurate categoriesPick the most specific primary category, then only the extra ones that truly describe the business.
  3. Keep hours and details currentUpdate holiday hours and any change of phone, address or website the same day.
  4. Add real photosUpload clear photos of your premises, work, team and products, and refresh them over time.
  5. Ask for reviews and answer all of themInvite genuine customers to review you, then reply to every review, good or bad.
  6. Keep your name and details consistentUse the same business name, address and phone on your website and everywhere else you are listed.

What Google says decides local ranking

Google explains this itself in its help page on how to improve your local ranking. It says local results are based mainly on three factors: relevance, distance and prominence. Everything else people write about ranking on Google Maps is either built on those three or is guesswork.

FactorWhat Google says it meansCan you change it?
Relevance"How well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for." Complete, detailed information helps Google match you to relevant searches.Yes, through the quality of your profile
Distance"How far each business is from the customer who's searching." If the searcher hasn't shared a location, Google estimates it from what it already knows.No. It depends on where the searcher is
Prominence"How well-known a business is." Google says links from other websites and the number of reviews feed into it, and that more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking.Partly, over time

Two more things from the same page are worth remembering. First, Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. Second, it keeps its ranking details confidential. Anyone who sells you a guaranteed spot on the map is selling something Google says does not exist.

That is also why this page makes no promises. Nobody outside Google can tell you exactly how much a photo, a reply or a category change will move you. What you can do is remove the avoidable reasons for being skipped, and the rest of this guide is about those.

What you can't control: distance and searcher location

Distance is the factor that surprises owners most. A customer standing two streets from your shop sees you high up. A customer on the other side of town may see a competitor first, even if your reviews are better. Google compares each business with where the searcher is, not with where you wish they were.

It also means that the order you see when you search for your own business is not the order your customers see. You are probably standing in or near your premises, which flatters you. Ask a friend in another neighbourhood to search the same words, or search from somewhere else, before drawing conclusions.

  • Searcher location: a result for "hairdresser" changes as someone walks through a town.
  • Search wording: "barber near me", "men's haircut" and "barber shop Leeds" can show different businesses.
  • Your service area: if you travel to customers, set your service area honestly instead of listing a shopfront you don't have.
  • Moving address: if you relocate, update the address; don't create a second profile. More on that below.

Accept this part and move on. You can't change the distance, so spend your effort on relevance and prominence, where the work pays off.

Relevance: make it obvious what you are

Relevance is about matching. Google has to decide whether your profile fits what someone typed, so give it clear, accurate material to match against.

Fill in the profile completely

Google's page on completing your Business Profile says customers are 70% more likely to visit a business with a complete profile. Treat that as a reason to leave no field empty: address or service area, phone, website, opening hours, description, services or products, and attributes that apply to you. If you are starting from nothing, our guide to adding your business to Google Maps covers the set-up, and the verification guide covers the step that unlocks editing.

Pick the right categories

Categories tell Google what kind of business you are, and they are one of the strongest relevance signals you control. Google's guidelines for representing your business say to use as few categories as possible to describe your core business, to choose the most specific ones, and to pick categories that describe what the business is, not what it offers or has. Don't use categories as keywords.

  • A pizzeria picks "Pizza restaurant" as primary, not just "Restaurant".
  • A dental practice that also does orthodontics may add that as a secondary category, because it is a real part of the business.
  • A garage doesn't add "Car wash" just because customers sometimes ask where one is.

Write a plain description and list your services

Say what you do, where, and for whom, in ordinary sentences. Don't repeat the same phrase over and over, and don't put phone numbers or web addresses in the description. If you offer specific services or products, list them in the matching sections of the profile instead of cramming them into the name or the description.

Keep hours and details accurate

A profile that shows the wrong hours sends people to a closed door, and those visits end badly for everyone. Check your regular hours, add special hours for bank holidays, and update your phone and website whenever they change. Our page on holiday hours on Google shows how.

Prominence: reviews, replies and being well known

Google's wording is that prominence means how well-known a business is, and that it draws on information from across the web, such as links from other websites. On reviews it is direct: more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking. It also says positive reviews and helpful replies can help your business stand out, and that replying shows customers you value their feedback.

Read that carefully. Reviews and ratings are named as helping ranking. Replies are described as helping a business stand out and showing you value feedback. Google doesn't say that a reply, by itself, lifts you up the list, so don't promise yourself that. What a reply does do is change how a stranger reads your profile, which affects who actually picks you.

Get more genuine reviews

Ask every happy customer, at the moment they are pleased, and make it easy: a short link or a QR code on the counter. Our guide to getting more Google reviews and the review request message templates give you wording that works. Google's rules allow you to ask for reviews, as long as you don't offer incentives or try to steer the rating or content.

Answer every review

Thank people for the good ones, and answer the poor ones calmly and specifically. Future customers read the replies as much as the reviews. Start with our guide on how to respond to Google reviews, which has example replies for every situation.

Be consistent across the web

Google lists links from other websites as part of prominence. Make sure your own website carries the same business name, address and phone as your profile, and that local directories, your social profiles and any press mentions match too. Different spellings of your name or an old address on a directory create doubt about which business is which. If you don't have a website, a simple one-page site with your details is still worth having.

Post updates and add photos

Google doesn't list photos or posts as ranking factors on its ranking page, so don't oversell them. They are still worth doing: they show people you are open and active, they answer questions before a customer has to ring, and a profile with fresh pictures looks alive next to one that hasn't changed for two years.

What not to do

Some tactics look like shortcuts and are really risks. Google's rules treat them as grounds for removal or suspension.

  • Don't stuff keywords into your business name. Google's guidelines say the name should match your real-world name, as used on your sign, website and stationery, and shouldn't include things like marketing taglines, hours, phone numbers, services or location details. Google warns that unnecessary information in the name can lead to suspension. "Smith's Plumbing - Cheap Emergency Plumber Leeds" is the kind of name that gets a profile suspended.
  • Don't buy or write fake reviews. Google's policy on prohibited and restricted content says fake engagement is not allowed and will be removed, and that reviews paid for directly or in kind are barred. See our pages on fake Google reviews and buying Google reviews.
  • Don't offer discounts or freebies for reviews. The same policy says incentivised reviews are not allowed. It also bars selectively asking only happy customers for reviews and discouraging negative ones.
  • Don't create duplicate profiles. Google says each business should have only one profile, because duplicates can disrupt how information appears on Maps and Search. If you moved, edit the existing one.
  • Don't list a fake address. A virtual office or a friend's shop you don't operate from is a misrepresentation, and Google can suspend the profile for it.
  • Don't abuse the categories. Adding a dozen loosely related ones doesn't make you more relevant; it makes you look less clear.

None of these is worth it. A suspended profile disappears from Maps and Search until it is reinstated, which is a far bigger loss than anything the shortcut might gain. If your profile has already vanished, our page on a business not showing on Google Maps walks through the likely causes.

Myths about ranking on Google Maps, stated carefully

  • "Paying Google moves me up." Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking.
  • "There is a secret trick." Google keeps its ranking details confidential, so anyone claiming an exact formula is guessing.
  • "More categories means more searches." Google's own guidance is to use as few as describe your core business.
  • "Replying to reviews makes me rank first." Google says replies help you stand out and show you value feedback. It doesn't say they are a ranking factor on their own.
  • "Posting every day boosts my ranking." Google's ranking page doesn't list posting frequency as a factor. Post when you have something worth saying.
  • "A review from a friend is fine." Reviews must reflect a genuine experience. Reviews from people with a conflict of interest are covered by Google's rating manipulation rules.
  • "I rank first when I search, so I rank first for everyone." Distance is part of the ranking, so your own view is not your customers' view.

A realistic order of work

If you only have an hour a week, do these in order. Each one fixes a gap Google can see.

  1. Check the basicsName, categories, address or service area, phone, website, hours. Fix anything wrong or missing.
  2. Clear the unanswered reviewsReply to every review you haven't answered, starting with the most recent and the lowest-rated.
  3. Add photosAdd real pictures of your premises, your work and your team. Replace any that are out of date.
  4. Start asking for reviewsAdd a short request at the end of each job, visit or order, with a direct link.
  5. Align your websiteCheck your site shows the same name, address, phone and hours as the profile.
  6. Review monthlySearch your own services from a different place, look at your profile, and fix what has drifted.

There is no promise attached to any of this. These are the things within your control, and they are what Google says its ranking is built on.

Frequently asked questions

Can I pay to rank higher on Google Maps?

No. Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking.

How long does it take to rank higher?

Nobody can give an honest number. Changes to details can show quickly, but prominence builds as reviews and mentions accumulate. Google doesn't publish timings.

Do reviews really affect my ranking?

Google says more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking. Review count and ratings feed into prominence.

Does replying to reviews improve my ranking?

Google says positive reviews and helpful replies can help your business stand out, and that replying shows customers you value feedback. It doesn't describe replies as a standalone ranking factor.

Can I put keywords in my business name?

No. The name should match your real-world name. Google's guidelines say unnecessary information in the name can lead to suspension.

Why do I rank first for me but not for my customers?

Distance is one of the three factors, and Google compares each business with where the searcher is. Your own location changes what you see.

How many categories should I use?

As few as describe your core business, starting with the most specific primary category. Google advises against using categories as keywords.

Will a complete profile guarantee a top spot?

No. Completeness helps Google match you to relevant searches, and Google says customers are more likely to visit a complete profile. It can't remove the effect of distance or competition.
